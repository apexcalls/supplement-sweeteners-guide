Why Supplement Brands Use Different Sweeteners in Gummies and Tablets

A gummy vitamin tastes sweet because it is mostly sugar. A chewable tablet tastes sweet because of a sweetener added to the mix. A capsule tastes of nothing at all, because it is swallowed whole. The sweetener a brand chooses is not a trivial detail: it affects the calorie count, the dental health of the user, the stability of the product, and the price. And the choice is different for every format, for reasons that have little to do with taste.

Gummies need sugar. A gummy is made by heating sugar, water and gelatin together, and the sugar is structural: it gives the gummy its body, its chew, and its shelf life. A sugar-free gummy is possible, using sugar alcohols like maltitol or sorbitol, but it behaves differently — it is softer, it dries out faster, and it can cause digestive discomfort in larger doses. A brand that makes a sugar-free gummy has solved a harder manufacturing problem, and the product usually costs more. A brand that makes a sugared gummy has taken the easier path, and the buyer should know how much sugar is in each serving: two gummies a day can add several grams of sugar to a diet that does not need it.

Chewable tablets use a different set of sweeteners, because a tablet cannot be mostly sugar and still hold together. The common choices are sorbitol, xylitol, and sucralose. Sorbitol is cheap and effective, but it can cause bloating in sensitive people. Xylitol tastes more like sugar and is kinder to teeth, but it costs more and is toxic to dogs — a fact the label rarely mentions. Sucralose is intensely sweet, so very little is needed, but some buyers avoid it on principle. The sweetener in a chewable is a compromise between cost, taste, and tolerance, and the label lists it in the "other ingredients" — where most buyers never look.

Capsules and tablets that are swallowed whole need no sweetener at all, and the best ones contain none. A capsule that is swallowed does not need to taste good, and adding a sweetener would only add cost and complexity. A brand that adds sweetener to a capsule is spending money on something the buyer will never taste, which is a sign that the brand is either careless or is using the sweetener for another purpose — to mask a bitter ingredient that leaks through the capsule shell, for example. Either way, the presence of a sweetener in a swallowable capsule is worth noticing.

The practical advice for a buyer is simple. For gummies, check the sugar per serving, and ask whether the sweet taste is worth the calories. For chewables, check which sweetener is used, and whether it agrees with you. For capsules, check that there is no sweetener at all — and if there is, ask why. A sweetener is not a sign of a bad product, but it is a sign of the choices the brand made, and those choices are worth understanding before you buy.
